Buyer’s guide

Best custom event website platforms for large organizers

October 27, 20269 minute readCustom Event Websites

For most large organizers the registration engine gets chosen first and the website gets whatever the platform happens to include. That order is backwards, because the website is the part forty thousand people see and the part the platform gives you the least control over.

This is a straight look at what the major event platforms cost, what their website builders actually do, and when it is worth building the public site separately. Pricing below comes from publicly available 2026 vendor roundups and is current as of October 2026. Enterprise pricing is quoted per account and moves, so treat these as a starting point for your own negotiation rather than a quote.

What changes when the event gets big

Small event websites have one job: tell people where to be. Past a few thousand registrations the site picks up five more jobs, and that is where platform builders start to strain.

  • Sponsors paid for placement. Someone wrote a check for a logo position and a dedicated page. A theme with a sponsor grid at the bottom is a problem you have to explain to them.
  • The agenda moves weekly. Sessions get added, speakers drop out, rooms change. Whoever edits that needs to do it without filing a ticket.
  • Per-registrant fees compound. A $9 per registrant charge is invisible at 200 people and $45,000 at 5,000.
  • Procurement and accessibility review the site. You will be asked for a VPAT or an accessibility statement, and template output varies.
  • It has to look like your brand. Not like the vendor's brand with your logo dropped in the corner.

Seven things to check before you sign

  1. Does the price move with headcount? Per-registrant pricing and per-ticket pricing are very different financial shapes from a flat seat licence.
  2. Can you touch the markup? Ask specifically whether you get HTML, CSS and JavaScript access or only a theme editor.
  3. Is a custom domain included? On several platforms extra domains are a paid add-on.
  4. What happens to the site when you leave? Most platform sites stop existing at the end of the contract. Decide now whether that matters.
  5. Can one account run several events? Flat seat pricing usually covers unlimited events. Per-event pricing does not.
  6. What is the accessibility position? Ask for the conformance report, not a sentence on a sales page.
  7. How fast can you change one speaker? Time that during the demo. It is the thing you will do most.

The platforms and what they cost

Cvent

The default for global enterprise programs. Licences are reported in the range of roughly $20,000 to $79,000 per year, plus $7 to $12 per registrant per event, plus implementation that public roundups put anywhere from $5,000 to $50,000. Nothing is published, so everything is a negotiation. It covers more ground than anything else on this list, including onsite, and that breadth is the reason to pick it. Cvent also now owns Splash, which was the design-led option in this category for years.

Bizzabo

Published entry point is around $17,999 per year, priced near $499 per user per month with a three-user minimum, billed annually. You cannot buy it for one event. It is built for teams running a year-round program, and the website and registration pages are a strong part of the product. Advanced branding is a paid add-on, so confirm what tier your brand requirements land in.

Swoogo

The one worth a close look if the website is the thing you care about. Its Professional plan is published around $11,800 per year, priced per admin seat with unlimited events and unlimited registrations, and no per-head fee. Crucially it gives you full HTML, CSS and JavaScript control on top of its themes, which is rare in this category. Extra custom domains, the mobile app, SSO and sub-accounts are add-ons.

Stova

Publishes a flat $4,995 Starter tier, which is unusual transparency for this end of the market. Higher tiers combine a base fee with per-registration charges. Strongest when you want the vendor running onsite operations as well as the software.

vFairs and RainFocus

Both quote only. Industry estimates put vFairs north of $25,000 per year. Both lean toward large managed conferences where you want a vendor team attached, and both are more about the attendee platform than the marketing site.

The mid-market tier

If your event is large by attendance but lean on budget, three options publish numbers. Accelevents lists plans at $7,500 and $13,500 annually. EventMobi starts around $3,000 per event or $8,900 per year, priced per attendee with add-ons like badge stock billed separately. Whova does not publish a full price but charges roughly 3% plus $0.99 per paid ticket, which again is a shape that grows with the room.

Where the word "custom" stops meaning custom

Every platform on that list markets a custom event website. In practice that almost always means a theme with your colors, your fonts and your logo, assembled from the same blocks every other customer gets. You can tell from the outside. Conference sites built on the same platform share a layout, a hero shape, a speaker grid and a footer, and attendees who go to four conferences a year see it.

Swoogo is the real exception because it hands you the markup. Everywhere else, if you want a layout the platform does not ship, the answer is no.

If the website is a line item on a platform contract, you get the platform's idea of a website. If it is its own project, you get yours.

The split that works: custom site, platform registration

The pattern large organizers land on more and more is to separate the two jobs. The registration platform does what it is genuinely good at, which is forms, payments, badges, sessions, check-in and reporting. The public-facing site is built from scratch and hands off to the platform at the moment someone clicks register.

What that gets you:

  • A site that looks like your organization and nobody else's
  • Sponsor placements wherever you sold them, not wherever the theme allows
  • A site that outlives the contract, because you own the files
  • Freedom to change registration vendors without rebuilding your public presence
  • Real control over page speed, structured data and accessibility, which matters when the site has to rank

The trade is one more vendor relationship and one integration point to keep tidy. For a single 200-person event that is not worth it. For a program that runs the same conference every year under a brand people recognize, it usually is.

A quick way to decide

Add up your per-registrant fees for next year. If that number is larger than the cost of building a site from scratch, the platform is charging you a design premium on top of a volume fee, and the split is worth pricing out.

How to choose, in order

  1. Start with the registration requirements, not the website. Sessions, badges, approval workflows, payment rules and reporting are the hard constraints. The site is the flexible part.
  2. Model the fees at your real headcount. Do it at your worst case, not your plan.
  3. Ask every vendor the markup question. The answer sorts the list fast.
  4. Price the website separately once. Even if you end up using the platform builder, you will know what you traded away.
  5. Check what survives the contract. Content, domain, attendee data, the site itself.

Questions people ask

What is the best event website platform for a large organizer?

It depends on which problem is biggest. Cvent covers the most ground for global enterprise programs and prices by quote. Bizzabo starts around $17,999 a year for teams running a year-round program. Swoogo is the strongest pick when the website itself matters most, at about $11,800 a year with full HTML, CSS and JavaScript control and no per-registrant fee. Stova publishes a flat $4,995 Starter tier.

How much do enterprise event platforms cost?

Published 2026 figures run from about $4,995 flat for Stova Starter and roughly $7,500 for Accelevents up to $11,800 for Swoogo, $17,999 for Bizzabo, and a reported $20,000 to $79,000 or more for Cvent licences. Cvent also adds $7 to $12 per registrant per event plus implementation fees. Confirm current pricing with each vendor.

Do event platforms really build custom websites?

Mostly they build themed websites. You get your colors, fonts and logo inside a layout the platform ships to every customer. Swoogo is the main exception because it gives you HTML, CSS and JavaScript access on top of its themes. If you need a layout the platform does not offer, you need the site built outside the platform.

Can I use a custom website with a platform like Cvent or Swoogo?

Yes, and large organizers increasingly do. The public site is built from scratch and the register button hands off to the platform, which keeps running forms, payments, badges and reporting. You keep the design and the files, and you can change registration vendors later without rebuilding the public site.

What does a custom conference website cost?

A site built from scratch runs from $350 for a single page up to $1,500 and above for a large multi-page build with ticketing handoff, speaker and sponsor pages and seating lookup. That is one time, with no subscription and no per-guest fee, which is a different financial shape from a platform licence.
